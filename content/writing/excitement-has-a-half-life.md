The first couple of days, the energy is real. You made the decision. You're telling people about it. Maybe it's a launch, a new year or a new business you've been sitting on for too long. Whatever it is, the start feels good, and that feeling costs you nothing.

Everybody can decide to start. Deciding is the easy part.

The first couple of weeks is where it gets challenging. The thing you were excited about starts asking for something back. It wants your mornings. It wants you to show up on the days you don't feel like it. And this is the part most people get wrong about quitting: they picture it happening at six months or a year, after some long, honest fight. That's rarely how it goes. Most people fall off in the first couple of months.

They don't lose a battle. They just stop.

## Novelty runs out on schedule

Excitement is generated by novelty, and novelty does not last. A new thing is new for a while, then it's just the thing you do. When the newness wears off, the excitement goes with it. It has a half-life, and the clock starts the moment you begin.

I'm not saying this to discourage anybody. I'm saying it because the drop is predictable, and anything predictable can be planned for. If you know the energy is going to fall, you stop being surprised when it does. You stop reading the dip as a sign that you chose wrong.

That last part matters more than people think. How you view the work decides what the long middle feels like. If you expected the beginning to last forever, the middle feels like failure. If you understood from day one that the excitement was temporary, the middle feels like what it is: the territory. It's where the work actually lives.

The beginning is a few days. The middle is most of it. If you only know how to operate in the beginning, you only know how to operate for a few days.

## What carries you past the drop

There are three things I've seen carry people past the point where the excitement leaves. In order: habit, principle and understanding.

**Habit** removes the daily decision. When something is a habit, you don't negotiate with yourself about whether today is the day. You don't wake up and run a vote. The decision was already made, so your energy goes into the work instead of into arguing about the work. Excitement asks you to choose every morning. Habit stops asking.

**Principles** are rules you set in a clear moment to govern your decisions in a cloudy one. And the cloudy moments are coming. You'll be tired. You'll be behind. Somebody will offer you a shortcut that looks reasonable at the end of a long week. That's the worst possible time to figure out what you stand for. You decide it beforehand, while your head is clear, so that when things get foggy the answer is already written down.

**Understanding** is the deepest of the three. It means knowing why this matters, what it builds toward and what it costs. What it costs to do it, and what it costs to not do it. Most people only count the first price. The second one is the one that keeps you going.

> You have to have a certain understanding of what you are working for every single day. How you view what you are doing and trying to accomplish matters.

Habit gets you out the door. Principles keep you honest once you're out there. Understanding is the reason you built the other two in the first place.

## Build it while the energy is high

Here's the timing piece, and it's the whole point.

The time to build the structure is while the energy is still high, before you need it. Most people do it backwards. They ride the excitement while it lasts, and only when it's gone do they start looking for discipline. By then they're trying to build the scaffolding in the middle of the fall.

Use the excitement for what it's good for. It's fuel. It's the one stretch where effort feels light, so spend that lightness on setting things up for the heavy days.

While you've still got that new-beginning energy, sit down and define three things.

Your daily non-negotiables. The small actions that happen whether you feel like it or not. Keep them specific and keep them small enough that a bad day can't break them.

Your principles. The rules you'll follow when you're tired, behind or tempted. Write them while you're clear, because you won't be clear later.

Your specific why. Not a slogan. The actual thing you're building toward and the actual price of walking away from it. If it could fit on anybody's wall, it's too vague to carry you.

Do this now, while it's easy. Because it won't stay easy, and that's fine. It was never supposed to.

> Excitement launches you. Understanding sustains you.

The beginning is free. Everything after it, you have to build for.
